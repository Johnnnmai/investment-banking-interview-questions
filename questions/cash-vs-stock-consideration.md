<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can weigh the consideration choice from both buyer and seller perspectives, including tax and signaling.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>Cash: certain value for sellers; buyer keeps all upside and risk; funded with cash on hand or debt.</li><li>Stock: sellers share future upside and risk; no new debt; dilutes buyer shareholders.</li><li>Cost: cash or debt is usually cheaper than issuing stock when the buyer&#x27;s after-tax cost of debt is below its earnings yield.</li><li>Tax: cash is generally taxable to selling shareholders; stock in a qualifying reorganization can defer tax.</li><li>Signals and execution: paying in stock may suggest the buyer thinks its shares are fully valued, and can require a shareholder vote or registration.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>From the seller&#x27;s side, cash gives certainty: they know exactly what they are getting and can walk away. Stock means they become shareholders of the combined company, so they share in the upside if the deal works and the downside if it does not. Taxes matter too: cash is generally a taxable event for the selling shareholders, while stock received in a qualifying tax-free reorganization can let them defer the tax.</p><p class=ans>From the buyer&#x27;s side, cash keeps all the future upside for existing shareholders and avoids dilution, but it uses up cash or adds debt, which raises financial risk. It is usually cheaper than stock when the after-tax cost of debt is below the buyer&#x27;s earnings yield, which is why cash and debt deals are more often accretive. Stock preserves the balance sheet and makes sense for very large deals or when the buyer&#x27;s shares trade at a high multiple.</p><p class=ans>There are softer factors. Paying with stock can signal that the buyer views its own shares as fully valued, and it ties the price to the buyer&#x27;s share price between signing and closing, so the parties negotiate a fixed exchange ratio or a fixed value with collars. Many deals use a mix to balance these trade-offs.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>What is the difference between a fixed exchange ratio and a fixed price in a stock deal?</b> A fixed ratio fixes the number of shares, so value moves with the buyer&#x27;s stock; a fixed price fixes value, so the number of shares moves.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Ignoring the seller&#x27;s tax position.</li><li>Forgetting that stock shares risk with the seller.</li><li>Saying stock is always cheaper because it needs no cash.</li><li>Ignoring signaling and price movement between signing and closing.</li></ul>

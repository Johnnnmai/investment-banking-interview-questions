<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can move a single change through all three statements in the right order, keep track of tax, and end with a balance sheet that balances.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>State the tax rate you are assuming (25% here).</li><li>Income statement: pre-tax income falls by the change; net income falls by the after-tax amount.</li><li>Cash flow statement: start with the lower net income, add back the non-cash depreciation; the net cash effect is the tax saving.</li><li>Balance sheet: cash changes by the tax saving, net PP&amp;E falls by the depreciation, retained earnings fall by the net income change.</li><li>Check that both sides move by the same amount.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>Assume a 25% tax rate. On the income statement, operating income and pre-tax income fall by $10, so net income falls by $7.50.</p><p class=ans>On the cash flow statement, net income is down $7.50, but depreciation is a non-cash expense, so I add back $10. Cash is up $2.50, which is the tax saving.</p><p class=ans>On the balance sheet, cash is up $2.50 and net PP&amp;E is down $10, so total assets are down $7.50. Retained earnings fall by $7.50, so liabilities plus equity are also down $7.50. It balances.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>How does a $100 goodwill impairment that is not tax-deductible flow through?</b> Net income falls $100, the non-cash charge is added back so cash is unchanged, and goodwill and retained earnings each fall $100.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Saying cash goes down. Depreciation does not use cash; the only cash effect is lower taxes.</li><li>Forgetting tax, so net income falls by the full $10.</li><li>Forgetting to reduce net PP&amp;E, so the balance sheet does not balance.</li><li>Not stating the tax rate up front.</li></ul>

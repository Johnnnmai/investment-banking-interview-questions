<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you treat impairment as a non-cash charge, handle the tax treatment correctly and balance the statements.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>Income statement: $100M impairment charge reduces pre-tax income by $100M.</li><li>Tax: goodwill impairment is usually not tax-deductible, so net income falls by the full $100M.</li><li>Cash flow statement: add back the $100M non-cash charge, so cash is unchanged.</li><li>Balance sheet: goodwill down $100M, retained earnings down $100M.</li><li>If the interviewer says it is deductible at 25%: net income down $75M, cash up $25M, goodwill down $100M, equity down $75M.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>Say the company records a $100M goodwill impairment. On the income statement, it is a non-cash charge that reduces pre-tax income by $100M. In most cases goodwill impairment is not deductible for tax purposes, so there is no tax saving and net income falls by $100M.</p><p class=ans>On the cash flow statement, net income is down $100M, but we add back the $100M impairment because it is non-cash, so cash from operations and total cash are unchanged. On the balance sheet, goodwill falls by $100M on the asset side, and retained earnings fall by $100M on the equity side, so it balances.</p><p class=ans>If the interviewer tells me to assume the impairment is tax-deductible at a 25% rate, pre-tax income falls by $100M and net income by $75M. The cash flow statement starts with net income down $75M, adds back $100M, so cash is up $25M from lower taxes. On the balance sheet, cash is up $25M and goodwill is down $100M, so assets are down $75M, and retained earnings are down $75M. It balances.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>Does an impairment change enterprise value?</b> Not directly; it is a non-cash accounting write-down that recognizes value already lost, although it can signal weaker expected cash flows.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Treating the impairment as a cash outflow.</li><li>Applying a tax benefit by default without stating the assumption.</li><li>Forgetting to add back the charge on the cash flow statement.</li><li>Reducing cash on the balance sheet when it is non-deductible.</li></ul>

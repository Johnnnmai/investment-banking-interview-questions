<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can reason about the direction and rough size of the change, and explain why terminal value drives most of it.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>Direction: value falls, because every future cash flow is discounted more heavily.</li><li>Terminal value usually dominates, so the effect is driven by it.</li><li>Gordon Growth: with g = 2%, going from 10% to 11% changes the terminal multiple of next-year cash flow from 1 / 8% = 12.5x to 1 / 9% = 11.1x, about 11% lower before discounting.</li><li>Discounting: higher rates also shrink the present value of all projected cash flows; the overall drop is often around 10-15% for a typical company.</li><li>Exit multiple method: terminal value itself does not change, only its present value, so the decline is smaller.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>The value goes down. A higher discount rate means each future cash flow is worth less today, and cash flows further in the future lose more value because the discounting compounds over more years.</p><p class=ans>The size depends mostly on terminal value, which often makes up the majority of a DCF. With the Gordon Growth method and a 2% long-term growth rate, raising the discount rate from 10% to 11% changes the denominator from 8% to 9%. Terminal value as a multiple of next year&#x27;s cash flow drops from 12.5 to about 11.1, roughly 11% lower, and then that smaller number is discounted at a higher rate too. Adding the smaller hit on the projected cash flows, a typical company might lose somewhere around 10% to 15% of its value, more if growth is high and the spread between discount rate and growth is narrow.</p><p class=ans>If terminal value comes from an exit multiple, the terminal value itself does not change; only the present value factor falls, so the decline is smaller. Over five years, discounting at 11% instead of 10% lowers the present value of terminal value by roughly 4%. That is why I always show a sensitivity table across discount rates and growth rates or multiples.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>Why does the effect get larger when growth is high?</b> The Gordon denominator is the spread between discount rate and growth; a 1% change matters more when that spread is small.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Saying value rises.</li><li>Claiming a 1% rate change gives a 1% value change.</li><li>Ignoring that terminal value drives most of the change.</li><li>Treating the exit multiple and Gordon Growth methods as equally sensitive.</li></ul>

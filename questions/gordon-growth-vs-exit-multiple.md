<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can choose between terminal value methods for a situation and use each to sanity-check the other.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>Exit multiple: terminal year EBITDA x a multiple from comps or precedents; common in banking and tied to market pricing.</li><li>Gordon Growth: final-year FCF x (1 + g) / (discount rate - g); grounded in long-run cash flow fundamentals.</li><li>Choose by context: exit multiple when good comps exist and for deal or LBO work; Gordon Growth for stable companies or when market multiples look distorted.</li><li>Cross-check: compute the multiple implied by the Gordon value and the growth rate implied by the exit multiple.</li><li>Red flags: implied growth above long-run economic growth, or an implied multiple far from current trading ranges.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>I would use both and let each check the other. The exit multiple method applies a multiple, usually EV/EBITDA from comparable companies, to the final projected year&#x27;s EBITDA. Bankers like it because it reflects how the market values similar businesses and it matches how buyers think about exit values. Its weakness is that it imports today&#x27;s market mood into a value years out.</p><p class=ans>The Gordon Growth method assumes free cash flow grows at a constant rate forever: terminal value equals final-year FCF times one plus g, divided by the discount rate minus g. It ties value to fundamentals but is very sensitive to the growth and discount rate assumptions.</p><p class=ans>So I cross-check. Say final-year free cash flow is $100M, EBITDA is $150M, WACC is 10% and long-term growth is 2%. Gordon Growth gives $102M divided by 8%, or about $1,275M, which implies roughly 8.5 times EBITDA. If comps trade at 8 to 10 times, that is reasonable. If an exit multiple of 14 times implied a perpetual growth rate of 5% or more, I would question the multiple, since a mature company cannot outgrow the economy forever.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>Which method is more common on the sell side?</b> Exit multiples are very common in banking, usually shown with a Gordon Growth cross-check.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Picking one method without checking it against the other.</li><li>Using a perpetual growth rate above long-run GDP growth.</li><li>Applying an exit multiple to the wrong year&#x27;s metric.</li><li>Forgetting to discount the terminal value back to today.</li></ul>

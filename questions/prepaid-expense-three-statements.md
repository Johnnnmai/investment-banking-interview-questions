<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can separate the cash payment from the expense recognition, move working capital correctly through the cash flow statement and keep the balance sheet balanced.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>At payment: no income statement effect.</li><li>Cash flow statement: the increase in prepaid expenses reduces cash from operations by $12M.</li><li>Balance sheet: cash down $12M, prepaid expenses up $12M; assets unchanged in total.</li><li>Over the year: $12M rent expense, so pre-tax income falls $12M and net income falls $9M at a 25% tax rate.</li><li>Cash flow: net income down $9M, plus the $12M decrease in prepaid, so cash from operations is up $3M; balance sheet: cash up $3M, prepaid down $12M, retained earnings down $9M.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>I would split it into the payment and the expense. When the company pays $12M upfront, nothing hits the income statement, because the rent has not been used yet. On the cash flow statement, prepaid expenses increase by $12M, which is a use of cash in operating activities, so cash falls by $12M. On the balance sheet, cash is down $12M and prepaid expenses are up $12M, so total assets do not change and it balances.</p><p class=ans>Over the year the rent is used up. The income statement shows $12M of rent expense, so pre-tax income falls by $12M and, at a 25% tax rate, net income falls by $9M. On the cash flow statement, net income is down $9M, but the prepaid asset decreases by $12M, which is added back as a source of cash, so operating cash flow is up $3M. That $3M is the tax saving.</p><p class=ans>On the balance sheet, cash is up $3M and prepaid expenses are down $12M, so assets are down $9M; retained earnings fall by $9M, so equity is down $9M and it balances. Looking at the full year combined, cash is down $9M and retained earnings are down $9M.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>Why does cash go up by $3M when the expense is recognized?</b> The expense lowers taxes by $3M, while the $12M itself was already paid earlier, so the non-cash expense is reversed through the prepaid change.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Putting the $12M rent expense on the income statement at the time of payment.</li><li>Forgetting the tax effect when the expense is recognized.</li><li>Recording the payment in investing activities instead of operating.</li><li>Showing cash falling by $12M again when the expense is recognized.</li></ul>

<h2>What the interviewer is testing</h2><p>Whether you can adapt the DCF to early-stage or turnaround companies and recognize its limits, instead of saying it cannot be done.</p>
<h2>Answer framework</h2><ol class=steps><li>Yes: a DCF values future cash flows, so negative near-term cash flow is fine if it turns positive later.</li><li>Extend the projection period, often to 10 years or more, until cash flows reach a steady state.</li><li>Model the drivers of the turn: revenue growth, gross margin, operating leverage and capex intensity.</li><li>Calculate terminal value only once the business is mature; most value will sit in terminal value.</li><li>Manage uncertainty: scenarios, sensitivity tables, a higher discount rate where justified, and cross-checks such as EV/revenue comps.</li></ol>
<h2>Sample answer</h2><div class=sample><p class=ans>Yes. A DCF values the cash flows a business will generate over its life, so negative cash flow today is not a problem as long as there is a credible path to positive cash flow. The negative years simply reduce value.</p><p class=ans>The main change is the projection period. For a high-growth company burning cash, a five-year forecast might end while it is still losing money, and a terminal value based on that year would be meaningless. So I extend the forecast, often to ten years or more, until margins and growth reach something like a mature steady state, and only then calculate terminal value. I build the forecast from drivers: customer or revenue growth, gross margin, how operating expenses scale, and capex and working capital needs.</p><p class=ans>The result is very sensitive, with most of the value in terminal value, so I would show scenarios, such as base, upside and downside, plus sensitivity tables on growth, margins and discount rate. I would also check whether the company has enough funding to reach profitability, since future dilution matters to equity value, and cross-check against EV/revenue or EV/gross profit multiples for similar companies.</p></div>
<h2>Likely follow-ups</h2><div class=fu><b>What if the company may never become profitable?</b> Then a DCF is not reliable; use liquidation value, comparable deals or option-style thinking, and say so.</div>
<h2>Common mistakes</h2><ul class=mist><li>Saying a DCF cannot be used at all.</li><li>Calculating terminal value while cash flows are still negative.</li><li>Ignoring funding needs and future dilution.</li><li>Presenting a single-point value without scenarios.</li></ul>
